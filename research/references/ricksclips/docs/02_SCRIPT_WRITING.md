# Script Writing

Scripts are written fresh for every video, from scratch. **Never** reuse a script structure other channels have already used on the
same topic: popular stories get copied by dozens of AI-slop channels with near-identical scripts. Use the hook bible
(docs/03_HOOK_WRITING.md) for every opening.

## The Ricks formula (from the style study)

Channel pivot: 15 s joke skits → **40–65 s narrated story explainers**. Top: Ancient Jar That Warned an Empire (32M),
Why Perfect Railroads Don't Work (24M), Job Nobody Wants in Japan (16M), Why Sailors Carried Storm Oil (14M). About 6 uploads/week.

1. **Hook** = an action with one absurd detail in sentence 1.
2. **"You see,"** right after the hook, and again when the explanation starts.
3. A **stubborn underdog** gets mocked in short quoted lines, then is proven right.
4. **Time-marker escalation**: "Weeks later…", "Then one night… then another… then all eight."
5. One **"Wait, …?"** mystery beat that voices the viewer's confusion.
6. One **everyday analogy** ("Ever blown on a bowl of soup?").
7. **Ending** = a short physical image. The last word ends the video. No CTA, no outro, no lingering shot.
8. Pace about **3.7 words/s** with almost no pauses.

## From the 19-video deep study (full detail: docs/07 section 5)

- **Topic lane**: clever tricks, loopholes and hidden mechanisms behind a dramatic mini-heist or mystery. **6 of 19 are money/gambling/lottery/betting**,
  a strong lane to stay in. Others: crazy-sounding old tech, psychology/brain, weird rules/institutions.
- **4 hook formulas**: (1) action + absurd detail (10 of 19) "He sawed gaps into a railroad every 39 feet." (2) paradox "Gambling is illegal in Japan,
  yet there's a gambling hall on almost every street." (3) you-address/challenge "Look at these two scratch cards." (4) rule/legend
  "The fastest gun in the West had one rule. Never draw first."
- **Mockery lines are jokes**, in short quotes: "Barbie's gonna love it in here." / "Hey, mirror man, maybe try wiping it."
- **A joke about every 10 s**, usually a hyperbolic comparison: "that horse lost a race to the milk cart, and it was pulling the milk cart."
- **Present tense** for the story, often **second person** ("Imagine your boss walks you past your desk…").
- **Specific numbers** everywhere (39 feet, 15 minutes, 47 scratches, 90%).
- 145–246 words, 3.67 words/s, max pause about 0.45 s.
- Ending: a physical image that calls back to the hook ("…by the time it spells out 9, nobody's laughing anymore.").
- **Write for the camera**: every clause must be actable (a verb the character can do, or a thing the camera can show). Abstract sentences can't be animated, so they make dead frames.

## Idea and title test (docs/07 S1: this decides 4M vs 32M)
- His craft is constant; topic and title decide the scale. Flops: "The Science Behind Slot Machines" (123K), "How Painkillers Actually Work" (309K).
  Hits: "The Ancient Jar That Warned an Empire" (32M), "Why Perfect Railroads Don't Work" (27M), "The Job Nobody Wants in Japan" (17M).
- **Never an explainer.** Every video is a story: a person, a trick, a twist. A money topic as an explainer flopped; money topics as stories hit.
- Title test: if the title could be a school lesson, rewrite it. Write 3 titles per idea using the hit patterns, and pick the most impossible-sounding.

## Practical adjustments

- Tone: **energetic but grounded**. Not fully comedic like Ricks. The voice is mid-range, not cartoony or high-pitched.
- **Controversy and numbers drive comments**: "he sold it *twice*", money figures, a moral grey zone people argue about.
- Length: 45–65 s is the sweet spot. Longer is fine when the story needs it, but every extra second needs higher retention.
  Cut 10–15 s if the middle drags.
- Explain *how* things started (how the rivals became rivals, how the trick began).
- Don't let the ending drag. Land the final image and cut.
- ElevenLabs v3 audio tags drive the performance: `[hushed]`, `[storytelling]`, `[beat]`, `[whispers]`, `[building]`, `[stunned]`, `[quick]`, `[wry]`.
  Write `...` for a breath pause.
- Film/book retellings: our own words, crediting the source in the caption, description and pinned comment.
